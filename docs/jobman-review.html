Jobman teardown and the hub's next step

How Jobman is built, screen by screen and rule by rule; where the hub is clunky today; 20 recommendations; a four-phase order to build them in. For George.

What this is built on. Read directly from Jobman: its 16 feature pages, 89 help-centre articles, the 80 lessons of the Academy's administrator course, the public API reference, release notes from January to 2 November 2026 (v2.6.30), and Jobman's own screenshots of the target calendar, agenda screens, capacity chart, job board, inventory, deliverables and kiosk. The video you sent is "End to End Demonstration of Jobman" (Jobman's channel, 1 hour 17 seconds, 26 June 2025, presented by Cam): its full transcript is in, from the copy you pasted on 7 November, and section 7 walks through it. The Gemini "handoff document" you sent with it is assessed in section 8. The hub findings come from running today's code on the 18 Notion jobs and screenshotting every screen at desktop and iPhone widths.

1. Short version

  • Jobman's scheduling engine is simpler than the hub's. A task has an estimated time, a "requires" list, and a date rule ("due same day as the previous task", "due 1 day after"). Dates chain forward from those rules and hours land on labour centres. The hub's schedule.ts already does the equivalent, with finish-to-start lags, squeeze mode and Aalbor's own flow baked in.
  • What makes Jobman feel sophisticated is everything around that engine: a to-do screen per team (agenda screens), a kiosk where the floor starts, finishes and flags work, status derived from ticks, a jobs-by-steps progress grid, alerts with photos, triggers that email when a task completes, and labels you scan on a cabinet. Those are products of screens and small rules, not of a cleverer scheduler.
  • The hub is clunky for one main reason: four long pages that show everything to everyone. Coming up on a phone is 33,759 pixels tall. One job is 14,607. Nothing is shaped for the person looking.
  • Jobman's inventory is the right blueprint for Phase 2 with the money removed. Catalogue items with SKU, supplier, minimum and reorder quantity; a materials list per job loaded by code and quantity (from Cabinet Vision or a CSV); auto-source from stock, then from stock on order, else to the order list; a picking list that takes stock off when the job starts; a ledger of movements; low-stock when on hand plus on order is below minimum.
  • Do it in four phases: make it light (2 weeks), make it role-shaped and self-updating (3 weeks), stock (4 to 5 weeks), then scanning. Stock depends on a materials list per job, and that only works once the job screen is something Christine wants to open.

2. How Jobman is put together

Jobman is an ERP for cabinet makers, built in Western Australia, in its "2.0" generation since 2023, released most weeks. Pricing in Australia: Small $250 a month (2 office and 5 kiosk licences), Medium $650 (6 and 15), Large (12 and 30); Cabinet Vision sync and the Appliances module cost extra. Everything below is how it works, not how it is sold.

2.1 The planning engine

  • Workflow → steps → tasks. One workflow per business is the recommended shape; job types switch tasks on and off. A step is tied to a job status, and the job moves to the next status by itself when every task in the step is complete. A task has an operation, a staff quantity, an estimated time, a date rule ("due same day as previous task", "due 1 day after", or set manually), a "requires" rule (all earlier tasks, none, or selected ones), and optional triggers.
  • Operations and labour centres. An operation (Cutting, Assembly, Install) is the reporting bucket. A labour centre is hours × staff per weekday, with zero on weekends and public holidays, linked to one or more operations and to a public-holiday group (the WA group in their example). Capacity adjustments are a named date range with new hours ("Jane annual leave", "Christmas shutdown"), and can add hours on a holiday the team works.
  • Target calendar. Every task is a block of hours on a day, drawn in its operation's colour, starting at 6:00 am by default. Month, week and day views; filters by status, type, member, member type, operation and task member, saved as presets; a lock icon on locked dates; split a block across days without exceeding the estimate. Moving a block asks two questions: recalculate the other tasks in this job, and ignore capacity. Managers lock jobs so nobody else moves them.
  • Capacity screen. A bar chart per day per labour centre: available hours as the pale bar, booked hours as the dark bar, over a date range you choose. It reads from the target calendar; you fix overloads there, not here.
  • Capacity check at job set-up. The demo's one engine moment (32:56): set the target date on a new job and Jobman tests it against the operations' hours and the labour centres' load, and says whether it is possible ("I would have had to have kicked this job off a month ago"). Or leave the date blank, give it the numbers, and it works forward to a date it can meet. Job types add or remove workflow allowances, Cam's example being two-pack doors that go out for painting and come back before assembly, which is exactly the hub's painting tick.
  • Members and member types. A job has members by type (Check Measurer, Drafter, Installer). A member type can be bound to operations, so adding Gus as Check Measurer puts him on every check-measure task automatically, and he gets the notifications for those.

2.2 The screens people live in

  • Agenda screens are the heart of it. One per team (Drafting, Install), a table of tasks in priority order: priority (Critical, Urgent, High, Normal), job number, description, task, date, time logged against estimate, a progress bar, and a message bar the manager types into ("Please ensure the job form post installation is filled out"). A red dot on the tab means something is overdue. Staff can be restricted to their own tasks; the screen has a full-screen mode for a factory TV and a share link; it works on a phone. Start, stop and complete from a right-click.
  • Progress chart. Jobs as rows, workflow steps as columns, each cell coloured: green complete, yellow due today, red overdue, grey on time or unscheduled, white no task. It exists to find unscheduled jobs and bottlenecks in one look.
  • Jobs list. Number, contact, description, status, a progress bar (red when behind), type; search and filters; finished jobs hidden by default. Critical operations can be shown as columns with their dates coloured blue, amber or red.
  • One job. Dashboard (status, progress bar, pending tasks, notes, activity), Info, Tasks as a Kanban of steps with task cards (date, member, tick; multi-select to complete or mark N/A), Resources (alerts, files, forms, materials, picking list, purchase orders, labour), Emails. The Kanban was rebuilt on 5 October 2026 for speed, colour coding (complete, overdue, over estimate) and touch panning.
  • Activity screens show who is clocked on and what they are working on. Timesheet-driven; not for the hub.

2.3 The floor: Kiosk

  • A separate licence and a separate, stripped interface: pick your name, type a four-digit PIN. Tiles for Clock on, Jobs, Tasks, Job alerts, Purchase orders, Inventory, Notifications, Print labels, Forms. Jobman sells a kiosk stand (Integra K4.0) with a touchscreen for the factory floor, but Cam's pitch is a phone, a cheap tablet, or the CNC's own screen, taken to site as well as used in the factory, and "if you can use Facebook on a phone, you can use this kiosk". Kiosk licences are priced "quite heavily" below office ones so that most of the team sit on them.
  • A task has Start, Pause, Stop, Set progress (a slider), Complete, Not applicable, Create alert, Files. Starting a task starts a timesheet entry; starting another stops the last. Their own guidance for internal jobs says kiosk users should start and stop but not complete, because status and reports hang off time.
  • Job alerts: a type (their examples: Damaged Stock, Drafted Incorrectly, Installer Damage, Job Incomplete, Material Defect, Site Damage, Near Miss), each type with responsible staff; a status (Reported, Assigned, Actioned, Completed); notes and photos; raised from the job or from inside the task; notifications to the people responsible; since June 2026 thumbnails, PDF and email. In the demo (41:11) an alert that needs a rerun becomes a work order inside the job, so the cost of the mistake is measured against that job rather than lost. Cam's other point: with files, drawings and specs on the kiosk, the floor stops interrupting the office to ask.
  • Forms: an installation checklist, risk assessment or delivery docket, built from groups of questions, filled on a phone with photos, auto-completed when the required answers are in, exported as a PDF with the company logo. Jobman's example is a one-page install checklist with three finished-kitchen photos and "Amazing kitchen. Client was super impressed." Cam calls forms his favourite kiosk feature and sells them as evidence: "five, ten, twenty photos", so a complaint a year later is answered with "when we left site, it was beautiful".
  • Deliverables: cabinets and panels as rows (number, name, size), populated from Cabinet Vision, with checkpoint columns you define (their example: Assembled, Loaded, Delivered), each ticked by scanning a QR label with a phone; hover shows who and when. Labels print from the kiosk on any label printer.
  • Triggers: on "task completed" (or N days before or after), send an email or an in-app notification to member types, staff or the client, with the job number and a link.

2.4 Stock

  • Catalogue: what you buy. Name, supplier (must be a contact), SKU, barcode, category, brand, range, finish, colour, size, unit, price, status (available to order or not), substitutes, minimum stock and reorder quantity, image and supplier link, with unit conversions for packs, rolls, pairs and square metres to sheets. Imported by CSV; Häfele and Wilson & Bradley catalogues sync by API, and an order sent to Häfele by API comes back with an order number, any price that differs from the catalogue, and an expected delivery date. Cam's case for the images: the wrong thing delivered because someone typed the wrong SKU "is probably costing you tens of thousands of dollars".
  • Inventory: what you have. Rows per receipt: item, location, bay, units, picked units, and a Job column when the row is allocated to a job. Locations are separate stocks with their own delivery address. The number that matters is available, not on hand: "if the shelf says 100, 80 may be assigned and only 20 are for stock" (53:54).
  • Job materials: what a job needs. From the quote, from a CSV of SKU and quantity, or from Cabinet Vision through JMSync (hardware from the report database; sheet counts only after nesting, from the optimiser's psnc_cv database; the Jobman job number must be the CV file name; the code sits in the SKU field or after a # in the description). Each line has a Source: None, Inventory, Stock in transit, or Purchase order.
  • Auto-source ("Source all"): for each line, take matching stock from inventory first (same SKU and attributes, oldest rows first), then from purchase orders not yet received, else add it to Pending Items with "order after" and "required by" dates. You can tell it not to take stock for a job far in the future. A summary says what it did and why. Cam's one-line version: "if it is in inventory, use it first; if it isn't, place it to bulk purchase ordering". Lines quoted as a price point ("a $20 handle") do not source; someone picks the exact SKU first. Bulk monthly orders land in stock and later jobs source from them.
  • Pending Items → purchase order: pending lines grouped by supplier become a PO; low-stock items join the list when on hand plus in transit is below minimum (only if supplier, SKU and minimum are set); the order quantity is the shortfall or the reorder quantity, whichever is larger. Since 20 October 2026 pending items sort by job. Receiving a PO, from the office or the kiosk, puts the stock in inventory, job-tagged if it was ordered for a job.
  • Picking list: the job's allocated rows; tick what was physically taken and it leaves inventory, with a review summary before it commits (October 2026). Stock ledger (since 7 October 2025): every movement as received, picked, sourced, adjusted, deleted, by staff member, with a stock-on-hand report beside it.

2.5 Platform

Browser app, no native phone app. Cloud file storage through Dropbox, OneDrive or Google Drive (needed before alerts can carry photos). Xero, MYOB and QuickBooks for contacts, invoices and bills. A REST API with OAuth and personal tokens, webhooks for job created, updated, status changed and more, and rate limits. Column customisation on every grid. Roughly weekly releases with public notes.

3. Why the hub feels clunky today

Measured on the 18 imported jobs, signed in as George, at 1280 px wide and on an iPhone 13.

  1. Everything on one page. Coming up is tiles, the week board, 41 overdue items and 14 decisions, all expanded. On a phone it is 33,759 pixels tall, about 40 screens. The board, which is the point of the page, is the first 15%.
  2. The same page for everyone. Phil, Gus, Christine and George all land on the same board. Phil's three factory steps this week sit among 60 other cards. Jobman's agenda screen shows a team its own tasks in priority order and nothing else.
  3. One job is a form, not a job. Thirteen steps appear twice: as date inputs with Confirmed tick boxes, then again as the Checklist. Then tasks, rework, details and history. On a phone it is 14,607 pixels. Jobman's job screen is five tabs, and the Tasks tab is a board of cards with one tick each.
  4. A single date change is a round trip. Move… opens the Reschedule screen, then "Work it out", then "Save 1 change", then back. Three taps and two page loads for one date. Jobman asks its two questions in a pop-up on the calendar and saves.
  5. Overdue is a chore, not a signal. Forty-one overdue steps in the snapshot. When ticking lags, every other signal (capacity, needs a decision, at risk) is noise, and the Friday clean-up falls on Christine. Jobman's answer is that the floor ticks from its own screen and the office reads the result.
  6. All jobs speaks in codes. CD, CM, DR, MI, F, P, PB, DL, IN, SM, SI, RW need a legend. The row for a job does not say what stage it is at. Jobman's jobs list says "Machining" and draws a progress bar.
  7. Every action reloads the page. A tick posts a form and re-renders the whole page; hub.js puts the scroll position back. On the factory wifi that is a visible pause per tick.
  8. Seven equal tabs. "One job" is a tab with a drop-down picker. Rework, Suggestions and Settings have the same weight as the board. Jobman has a search box on every list.
  9. Nothing reaches out. The hub waits to be looked at. The only email it sends is the sign-in link and one per bot batch. Jobman notifies the people responsible when an alert is raised, a task is assigned, or a trigger fires.

None of these are faults in the rules or the data. The database, the roles, the change log, dependencies, capacity and the Buildertrend suggestions are solid and should not be rebuilt. The step-change is in the screens and in where status comes from.

4. Recommendations

R1 to R8 are production planning. R9 to R13 are the floor. R14 to R20 are stock. Each says what Jobman does, what to build, and what changes under the hood.

Production planning

R1Split Coming up into Board and Attention

The board stays the home page and ends with the week. Overdue and Needs a decision move to an Attention screen, grouped by job (one line per job with a count, tap to expand), sorted by install date. The tiles link to it. The phone page drops from 40 screens to about 6.

Under the hood: views only. No migration.

R2My day, and a Factory screen

Jobman's agenda screen and kiosk, without the clock, the PIN or the licence. A Doer lands on My day: their steps for today and this week in order, each with the job, the suburb, the date, one big Done button, a note box and Flag a problem (R9). Gus sees check measures. Phil and the factory sign-in see factory, delivery and doors back. Christine sees drafting, drawings and orders, plus the Attention count. A one-line message from George or Christine sits at the top, as Jobman's message bar does. The same screen has a TV mode (bigger type, no header, refreshes itself) for the factory wall, showing the whole factory's week. Steps are matched by the owner already on each step, so the only new data is a "works on" list per person in Settings. One more thing from the demo: the floor's constant question is "where are the drawings?", and Cam sells the kiosk on ending that interruption. A Drawings link per job (the Dropbox or Buildertrend folder, a web address only, no files in the hub) shown on My day answers it.

Under the hood: people.areas (text array), jobs.drawings_url, app_settings.board_message, routes /me and /factory, a per-role landing page.

R3One job as a timeline, not a form

One row per step: name, owner, date range, a status pill (Done, Confirmed, Provisional, Overdue), a Done tick, and the date itself as the tap target. Tapping a date opens a small box on the spot with the date picker and the three choices (move everything after, keep install, only this), the same box the drag already uses. Remove the separate Checklist panel; the Dates panel becomes it. "Save dates" and "Save and confirm all" stay for bulk edits, behind "Edit all dates". A progress bar under the title, as Jobman draws it.

Under the hood: views and hub.js. The quick reschedule route already exists.

R4Pipeline grid

Jobman's progress chart, which suits a Monday meeting better than its Kanban. Live jobs as rows, the stages as columns (Drafting, Check measure, Drawings, Materials, Factory, Painting, Delivery, Install, Stone), each cell coloured: done, due this week, overdue, not yet, not applicable. The job's current stage is the first column not done. A tap on a cell opens that step. It answers "where is every job" in one look, which the week board cannot, and it exposes jobs with no dates.

Under the hood: a view over existing data. Stage = first step in FLOW not done.

R5Capacity by area in hours, with a 6-week load chart

Jobman's labour centre: hours × people per weekday, zero on weekends and holidays, a public-holiday group, and dated adjustments. Replace the flat "2 kitchens a week" with four areas (Factory, Install crew, Check measures, Drafting), each with hours per weekday and who is in it. A job's factory and install days become hours (days × hours per day, so today's numbers carry over); rework hours already count. The load chart is Jobman's: six weeks, one row per area, a pale bar for available and a dark bar for booked, per day, red when over. Capacity adjustments replace blocked days: a date range, an area or a person, and the new hours (half day, one person off). Whole-factory closures stay as today. NSW holidays are already in the database.

Under the hood: labour_centres, capacity_adjustments, capacity() in hours; blocked_days kept as the all-closed case.

R6Pinned dates

Jobman's locked target date, with its padlock icon. A Pinned tick on a step (install, usually, once the client has the date) that no shift, squeeze or quick move changes without "Move anyway". Confirmed keeps meaning "matches Buildertrend"; Pinned means "promised".

Under the hood: steps.pinned, honoured in reschedule().

R7Propose the first week with room

Jobman does half of this: at set-up it tests the target date against the labour centres and says whether it is possible, or works forward from the numbers to a date it can meet (32:56 in the demo). It does not offer the next week with room. When a new job is proposed, show the factory and install weeks it would land in with their load from R5, and offer "next week with room" as a one-tap alternative. Needs a decision already catches overbooking afterwards; this catches it at entry.

R8Fewer tabs, and a job search

Board · Pipeline · My day · Jobs (the Gantt, with a stage column and full step names in a hover title) · Attention · More (Rework, Suggestions, Stock, Settings). A search box in the header finds a job by number, client or suburb and opens it, as every Jobman list has. Drop the One job tab.

The floor

R9Flag a problem

Jobman's job alert. From My day or any step: a type with a responsible person per type (Materials short → Angela; Drawing query → Christine; Site not ready → George; Damaged → Phil; Other), one line, an optional photo from the phone camera. It lands on Attention for the responsible person and the Editors, with the step and who raised it; status Open, Looking at it, Fixed; can be turned into a rework or closed with a note. Rework stays for work that has to be done again; a flag is for "I'm stuck".

Under the hood: issues and issue_types with hub_guard and hub_log; Doers may add; photos on Render disk or R2, 1 MB cap, resized on upload.

R10Partial updates without a page reload

A tick, a flag or a date move should change the row, not the page. Keep the server-rendered forms (they work without script) and add one small layer in hub.js: on submit, post the form, receive the re-rendered panel, swap it in. No framework; the rule in CLAUDE.md stands. This removes most of the "clunk" by itself.

Under the hood: routes answer with the panel's HTML when the request asks for it, full page otherwise.

R11Nudges by email, then a daily digest

Jobman's triggers, reduced to four fixed ones instead of a builder. Resend is already wired. A Monday 7 am "your week" per person from My day. A daily 7 am note to the owner of any step that went overdue yesterday (not to Christine). A flag notification to the responsible person. A "materials in" note to Phil when the factory sign-in ticks it. Each has one link to the step. Push notifications on phones can follow if email proves too slow.

Under the hood: a Render cron like the backup, people.email_digest on or off.

R12Job sheet with a QR code

The cheap half of Jobman's deliverables and kiosk labels. A printable one-page job sheet (number, client, suburb, finish, dates, cabinet count) with a QR code that opens My day for that job on a phone. Stuck on the trolley, it means Phil ticks factory done and delivery done from the trolley, not from a list. Cabinet-level labels and checkpoints are Phase D if the sheet proves useful.

R13Done with photos on install

Jobman's install checklist form, cut to what matters. Ticking Install cabinets done asks three things: gaps adjusted (yes/no), site left clean (yes/no), and up to three photos. They sit on the job and in History, and the Monday email can show "installs finished last week" with the photos. No PDF, no template builder.

Under the hood: step_photos, the same upload path as R9.

Stock (Phase 2)

This is Jobman's inventory design with the money removed. The brief's section 6 maps onto it almost line for line.

R14Catalogue

Load the 183 Notion lines. Each item: code (the Cabinet Vision product code, which is what makes R15 automatic), name, kind (board always stocked, board to order, standard hardware, per-job hardware, consumable), unit (sheet, box, each, metre), supplier, supplier lead days, minimum, reorder quantity, bin label, active. Jobman only flags low stock when supplier, SKU and minimum are all set; the hub's edit form should say so on the row. Substitutes and prices are left out. An optional picture per item is worth keeping, Jobman-style as a link to the supplier's image rather than an upload, because Cam's "wrong thing delivered" problem is real and cheap to prevent.

R15Materials per job, from Cabinet Vision by file

Jobman's JMSync sends only quantity and product code, keyed by job number, and its generic driver is a two-column CSV of SKU and quantity. Do the same without the Windows app: Christine exports the CV material report for the job and drops the file on the job's Materials panel. Codes are matched to the catalogue; unknown codes are listed for a one-time match, remembered after that. Sheet counts are right only after nesting, so the panel shows "from the nested file" or "estimated" per line. Hand entry stays for one-offs. Later, a small reader for the optimiser database can replace the export.

Under the hood: job_materials (job, item, quantity, source: stock / order / none, nested: yes/no), code_aliases.

R16Source: stock first, then on order, else the list

Jobman's auto-source, without the dollar side. One button on the job's materials: for each line, allocate from stock on hand (oldest first), then from stock already on order, and put the rest on the order list with an "order by" date (factory date minus supplier lead days, in working days). A tick box stops a far-off job taking stock a nearer job needs. The summary says what was allocated and what was not, as Jobman's does.

R17Demand against supply, by factory date

Six weeks out, per board colour and per hardware line: needed (jobs by factory date), on hand, on order with expected date, and the gap. This is the "Jones needs 6 Satin White on 3 Nov, you have 2, order 4 by 29 Oct" line from the brief, and it is where stock pays for itself.

R18The Monday order list

Jobman's Pending Items, stopping before the purchase order. One list grouped by supplier, sortable by job: shortfalls from R16 and R17, per-job hardware not in stock, standard items flagged low (R20). Angela ticks Ordered and types the expected date; the item moves to on order; receiving (the factory sign-in, from a phone) ticks In, fully or partly. Nothing on hand or on order can be listed twice. No order numbers, no prices.

Under the hood: orders (item, quantity, job or stock, ordered on, expected, received on, received quantity).

R19Stock ledger, picking, counts and offcuts

Jobman's ledger and picking list. On hand is never typed; it is the sum of movements: received, picked for a job, counted, adjusted with a reason, offcut logged. Every stock line shows two numbers, on hand and available (on hand minus what R16 has allocated to jobs), because if the shelf says 100 and 80 are assigned, you have 20. Picking happens when the factory step starts: the job's allocated lines appear with tick boxes and a review summary, and what is ticked leaves stock. Phil's Friday screen lists the stocked boards with the last count and a number box each; a difference is logged as a count movement. The quarterly stocktake is the same screen for every active item. Offcuts of non-stocked colours over half a sheet are logged with colour, size and location and shown on R17 when that colour is next needed.

R20Running low, from the bin, and readiness on the job

Two-bin for the standard range: each bin label carries a QR code; scanning it opens one button, "Running low", which puts the item on the order list with who and when, and shows what is on order already so nobody flags twice. Once R15 to R18 exist, "Materials in" stops being a tick someone remembers: the job shows ready or short (with the short lines and their expected dates), and a job whose factory date is inside a shortfall shows on Attention and on its Pipeline cell. This is the single biggest planning gain from stock, and it is why stock comes after the planning screens, not alongside them.

5. Phasing

Why not everything at once: the team is two to three weeks into Phase 1, and the brief's success test (board current every Monday, Overdue cleared each Friday) has not been run yet. The screen changes need no new data and get adoption; the floor changes need the screens; stock needs a materials list per job, which needs a job screen Christine opens willingly and a Cabinet Vision export habit. Each phase ends with a week of use before the next starts. Weeks are build effort, not calendar time.

Phase A: make it light (about 2 weeks)

R1 Board and Attention · R3 One job as a timeline · R8 tabs and search · R10 partial updates · R6 Pinned dates.

One migration (pinned). What you notice: the phone pages are short, a tick or a date move happens where you are, and you find a job by typing its number.

Phase B: role-shaped and self-updating (about 3 weeks)

R2 My day and the Factory screen · R4 Pipeline grid · R9 Flag a problem · R11 nudges · R5 capacity in hours with the load chart · R7 first week with room · R12 job sheet with QR · R13 install photos.

Migrations: people areas, issues, labour centres and adjustments, photos. What you notice: Phil and Gus open the hub on their own because it shows only their work; the Monday meeting runs off the Pipeline grid; overdue nudges go to the person, not to Christine; capacity says hours and names; the factory TV shows the week.

Phase C: stock (about 4 to 5 weeks)

R14 catalogue (week 1) · R15 materials per job and the CV upload (week 2) · R19 ledger and Phil's first count (week 2 to 3) · R16 sourcing, R17 demand against supply and R18 the order list (week 3 to 4) · R20 bins and readiness on the job (week 5).

Start with the catalogue and Phil's first count, so there is a true on-hand figure before any demand is shown. The order list goes live only when four weeks of jobs have materials loaded, or the shortfalls are fiction.

Phase D: later, if the job sheet earns it

Cabinet-level labels and scanning at assembled, loaded, delivered, installed (Jobman's deliverables). A reader for the Cabinet Vision optimiser database. Two-way Buildertrend. Push notifications.

6. What not to copy from Jobman

  • Timesheets as the engine. Jobman's status, progress bars, agenda "time" columns, activity screens and reports all hang off time logged against tasks, and its own guidance is that kiosk users should start and stop tasks but not complete them. Aalbor said no timesheets. A Done tick, a flag and a photo are enough signal, and they take one tap.
  • Configurable workflows, operations, statuses, member types, alert types, triggers, forms and templates. They are how Jobman serves every manufacturer; they are also why its set-up guide is a 27-step flowchart and an 80-lesson course. The hub's flow is Aalbor's flow, in code, with tests. Where a list is needed (flag types, areas), keep it short and seeded.
  • The hour-by-hour target calendar with 6:00 am blocks and split segments. Right for a machine shop; too fine for two kitchens a week. Day-level dates with hours for capacity is the right grain.
  • A separate kiosk interface and licence. The hub's Doer role is the kiosk; My day is its screen; the phone in their pocket is the hardware.
  • Purchase orders, supplier pricing, job costing, catalogue images and the supplier integrations. Out of scope, permanently (brief, section 1).
  • A desktop sync app and cloud-storage dependencies. A file upload does the same job for one drafter; photos go straight to the hub's own storage.

8. The Gemini handoff document

Gemini's "Comprehensive Handoff Document" is a generic ERP build spec written from the video, not a reverse-engineering of Jobman. Its stack (a React single-page app, WebSockets, a JWT gateway), its table definitions and its API routes are invented. Jobman's real API lives under /api/v1/organisations/{organisation}/…, models a job as steps containing tasks (estimated time in minutes, a locked flag on the target date, labour centres and members per task, a requires rule), signs in by OAuth or personal token, and fires webhooks per event. None of the document's routes (/kiosk/auth, /scheduling/capacity, /inventory/allocate, /procurement/batch, /suppliers/edi/order) exist there, and its Häfele payload with an "api_secret" field is made up.

Three of its rules are right, and match both the documentation and the transcript: capacity load per work centre per day against available hours (Jobman's capacity chart); net procurement equals required minus available, where available is physical stock minus allocated stock (Cam's "100 on the shelf, 80 assigned"); and a shop-floor alert with a type, notes and photos. Those are already R5, R16, R19 and R9.

The rest is wrong for the hub. Two of its three pillars, parametric quoting with sheet multipliers, and supplier EDI ordering with job profitability, are things the brief excludes for good. Its "dynamic schedule recalculation from actual labour hours" is the timesheet engine section 6 says not to copy. Its "alert triggers a RERUN task" is a guess at something Jobman does by hand (a work order). Its schemas are thinner than the hub's own: no change log, no roles in the database, no provisional dates, no rework, no suggestions.

Verdict: do not build from it, and do not hand it to another session as a brief. Keep its two formulas as a cross-check on R5 and R16. The blueprint for the hub is this document plus the brief and the build plan.
